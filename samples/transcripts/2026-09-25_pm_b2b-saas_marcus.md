**Nina** [00:00:06]: Hey Marcus, thanks for jumping on. Is it okay if I record?

**Marcus** [00:00:10]: Sure. Fair warning, I've got about twenty minutes before a planning thing, so I'll talk fast.

**Nina** [00:00:17]: Twenty is plenty. Quick framing: I'm researching an early idea called Throughline, a workspace for building personas, journey maps, hypotheses and roadmaps from research, with evidence linked to interview quotes. I want to understand how you actually do your job, and I'm very happy to hear this is a bad idea.

**Marcus** [00:00:38]: Oh good. I might take you up on that.

**Nina** [00:00:42]: Go for it. Tell me about your role.

**Marcus** [00:00:45]: I'm a senior PM at Brightforge. We make scheduling and resource-planning software for mid-size construction firms. B2B SaaS, about two hundred and forty customers, mostly annual contracts. I own the scheduling module. Squad is six engineers, a designer, and a part-time data person. Before this I was at two other SaaS companies, one enterprise, one tiny.

**Nina** [00:01:15]: And where do you spend your time, tool-wise?

**Marcus** [00:01:19]: Roadmap lives in Aha!, that's the company standard, but honestly my real working roadmap is a Linear project with a view I trust. Notion for specs and meeting notes. Slack, obviously. Amplitude for usage. Gong recordings from sales calls, I listen to those at one and a half speed on the train. I'd say eight hours a week in Linear and Aha!, maybe four in Notion, and then whatever's left in the calendar.

**Nina** [00:01:52]: Okay, I want to start with personas, since that's central to what I'm exploring. Do you use them?

**Marcus** [00:01:58]: Ha. Here we go. So, we have personas. Four of them. Our head of design commissioned them in a workshop in, I think, early 2025. There's a lovely Figma file. Dave the site superintendent, Carla the project controller. I will be honest, I have opened it twice since. Both times to copy a headshot for a slide.

**Nina** [00:02:25]: So, what happened to them?

**Marcus** [00:02:28]: They're dead artifacts. That's my phrase. Everyone loves making them. The workshop is great, sticky notes, snacks, high fives. Then the file goes in a folder and nobody opens it. Our customers are not "Dave". Our customers are fifty-person subcontractors with a very specific scheduling headache, and I know them by name, because I talk to them. I've got a Slack channel with customer champions. That's my persona. A real person who replies in forty minutes.

**Nina** [00:03:05]: How about journey maps?

**Marcus** [00:03:08]: Same. We did one for onboarding, last year, I think, a consultant did it. It's a gorgeous poster. We printed it. It is on a wall in the Berlin office and I've never seen anyone look at it. The actual journey I look at in Amplitude: where do people drop between sign-up and first published schedule. That's a funnel, that's measurable. A map with emotions, little smiley faces, I can't act on.

**Nina** [00:03:40]: What would make a persona or journey map useful to you?

**Marcus** [00:03:44]: Honestly, if it updated itself and I never had to look at it, and it just changed what showed up in my roadmap. But that's not a persona, that's a recommendation engine. I don't think the artifact is the point. The point is decisions. Does it change a decision? If not, why maintain it.

**Nina** [00:04:08]: How often should they be updated, in your view, if you kept them?

**Marcus** [00:04:12]: If you ask a design person, quarterly. In reality they're updated never. Ours has been updated zero times in eighteen months. And I don't think that's a failure. It means they weren't load-bearing.

**Nina** [00:04:30]: Okay. And hypotheses? Do you write them down?

**Marcus** [00:04:34]: Yes, in the roadmap item itself. Each initiative in Linear has three lines at the top: the problem, the bet, the metric. "Customers waste time re-sequencing after a delay. We believe an auto-resequence button reduces re-planning time. We'll look at median time from delay logged to schedule republished." That's a hypothesis. I don't need a separate hypothesis tool. It sits next to the work.

**Nina** [00:05:10]: Where does the evidence for the problem come from?

**Marcus** [00:05:14]: Calls with customers, Gong snippets, support tickets, usage. I paste two or three representative quotes into the Linear ticket and a link to the call. Two or three. That's enough. I've heard BAs I've worked with want every claim to link to a source, with IDs and a matrix. I think that's overkill. It slows you down, and no one reads the chain. If a decision is wrong we find out from the metric, not from the footnote.

**Nina** [00:05:50]: That's a clear position. Do you run research interviews regularly?

**Marcus** [00:05:55]: I do about six customer calls a month, so, call it eighteen, twenty a quarter. Plus there are sales calls I listen to. Not what a purist would call discovery, but it's real.

**Nina** [00:06:12]: And who owns the roadmap at Brightforge?

**Marcus** [00:06:15]: Me. Product. Fully. We do have a business analyst on the implementation side, she's great, but her job is to translate customer requirements into configuration for onboarding. She does not own roadmap. I've worked at a place where the BA owned the roadmap and it turned into a requirements list. A roadmap is a strategy argument. That's PM work.

**Nina** [00:06:50]: Tell me about presenting the roadmap to executives. How does that go?

**Marcus** [00:06:55]: Oh, that's most of my stress, honestly. Every quarter there's a roadmap review with the CEO, CFO, head of sales, head of customer success. I have forty-five minutes. I show a now-next-later slide, three themes, no dates. The CFO wants dates. Sales wants dates. I give them confidence bands instead: committed, likely, exploring. It's a negotiation every time.

**Nina** [00:07:35]: What do you wish you had at that meeting?

**Marcus** [00:07:39]: A one-pager that says here's what we're betting on and here's the one number that tells us if it's working. And honestly, a way to answer "why this and not that" in thirty seconds. Sales will push, "my customer asked for X, why isn't it in", and I'd love to point at something and say, here are the nine customers who asked, here's the revenue at stake. I do that in a spreadsheet right now. A spreadsheet I rebuild every quarter. That's maybe six hours the week before.

**Nina** [00:08:15]: Interesting, so the demand is evidence feeding the prioritization conversation, rather than a persona.

**Marcus** [00:08:20]: Right. Show me demand, not archetypes.

**Nina** [00:08:28]: Okay, let me test the actual idea. A tool takes interview transcripts and auto-drafts the themes, quotes, maybe a persona. Your reaction?

**Marcus** [00:08:38]: The theme-and-quote part I could use. Gong already does a flavor of it. If a tool gave me, I don't know, "fourteen of your last thirty calls mention re-sequencing after rain delays," with links, I'd be into it. Auto-persona? I'd never open it. The tool can make it, I just won't read it. So I wouldn't pay for it. I trust the AI summary to the extent it's a search engine. I don't trust it as the truth, but I don't trust my own sticky notes either, if I'm honest.

**Nina** [00:09:20]: What about the tool category, a dedicated discovery workspace, versus your stack?

**Marcus** [00:09:25]: My stack works. Linear plus Notion plus Amplitude plus Gong. It's not elegant. But I've done the "one tool to rule them all" thing; I've adopted three of them. A new tool is a new silo. It's another login, another thing people forget to update, another license I have to justify to finance in January. Last year finance made us cut seven tools. We spend, I think, around nine thousand dollars a year on the roadmap tool alone for the product org, and there was a whole conversation.

**Nina** [00:10:05]: What would have to be true for you to consider it?

**Marcus** [00:10:09]: It'd have to live inside Linear, or sit on top of it so that I never go there. If it's a destination, I won't go. If it's an automation that puts the right customer quotes on my ticket, great.

**Nina** [00:10:30]: A tangent. You said you've adopted tools before. Why did they fail?

**Marcus** [00:10:35]: Mostly because nobody owned the upkeep. The tool is great the first month, because someone, usually me, loads it with data. Then I get busy and it's stale. And a stale tool is worse than no tool, because people trust it. Maybe the reason I hate personas is the same reason: stale, but authoritative-looking.

**Nina** [00:11:05]: That's a good insight. So what is the actual job you'd hire a tool for?

**Marcus** [00:11:10]: Two. One: make my roadmap defensible in the room, fast. Two: stop me from building something nobody asked for, which happens maybe once a quarter and costs a sprint. Those two.

**Nina** [00:11:30]: If I forced you, what would you pay?

**Marcus** [00:11:34]: Per seat, for just me? Probably ten, twelve dollars a month, expensed on a card, no questions. Thirty, I'd have to fight for it. The thing is, I don't think of it as a seat. The product org is nine people. We'd want it as a team plan. If the team total is under, I don't know, a hundred a month, I'd just do it without asking. Over three hundred, finance gets involved, and I'd say no.

**Nina** [00:12:10]: Fair. Anything about the current setup you'd praise?

**Marcus** [00:12:14]: Linear. It's fast. It's opinionated. Engineers don't hate it, which is the miracle. And Amplitude, when set up properly, is the closest thing we have to the truth. I'd keep those until they pry them out.

**Nina** [00:12:35]: Is there a place where research does feed the roadmap smoothly?

**Marcus** [00:12:39]: When I talk to the customer myself and bring the quote to the squad meeting. That's it. It's me, a human. Not a system.

**Nina** [00:12:55]: One last area. Compliance or auditing needs in your customers' world?

**Marcus** [00:13:00]: Not for our docs, no. Our customers do safety compliance on site, but that's their problem and a feature in our product, a safety-checklist module. Not our process.

**Nina** [00:13:15]: Okay. Thanks, Marcus. Anything I haven't asked?

**Marcus** [00:13:19]: Only this. If you're building this, don't build a place for artifacts. Build something that changes what I do on Monday morning. Artifacts are the hobby; decisions are the job.

**Nina** [00:13:32]: Strong line. Thank you very much.

**Marcus** [00:13:35]: Good luck. I'm curious how it goes, honestly. Even if I'm not your customer.
